\begin{abstract}
	The aim of this guide is to provide a practical overview of fabricating a home-made Printed Circuit Board (PCB) using a UV exposure unit and chemical etching, rather than a Computer Numerical Control (CNC) milling machine. This guide covers the process from the completed layout design through to component assembly. It does not cover the layout design rules required for home-made PCB manufacturing, nor is it an exhaustive manufacturing manual. Please follow this guide at your own risk.
\end{abstract}
%
\section{Photolith printing}
The first step in PCB manufacturing using a UV exposure unit and chemical etchant is printing the photoliths with the negative image of each layer onto transparent film (acetate)\footnote{If printing photoliths at home, ensure you use transparency film designed for laser printing; standard acetate sheets will warp or melt inside a laser printer.}. These films protect the photoresist layer when the copper-clad board is placed in the exposure unit.

It is important to print two photoliths for each layer and overlay them to achieve adequate opacity for the circuit traces. This double-layer approach ensures that UV light does not bleed through the printed areas. The sheets can be aligned by trimming the corners of one transparency and fastening each corner with clear adhesive tape. Next, align and tape three corners of one layer's photolith pair to the other layer's pair. Ensure both sides are accurately registered. In \autoref{fig:photo}, the aligned, overlaid photolith pairs for both board layers are shown for two example designs.

\section{Copper plate UV exposure}\label{section:insol}
This section explains how to expose the copper-clad board\footnote{Ensure that you use presensitised photoresist copper-clad laminate.} using the photolith masks. If the initial copper sheet is larger than the required board dimensions, a guillotine shear should first be used to trim it to size. In \autoref{fig:shear}, the copper board is shown placed in the shear, ready to be cut.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth]{figures/process_cutting_shear.jpg}
	\caption{Guillotine shear with the copper board prepared}
	\label{fig:shear}
\end{figure}
%
After obtaining a board of the desired dimensions, slide it between the two registered photolith pairs and place the assembly inside the exposure unit, as shown in \autoref{fig:insol}. Ensure that the copper board is centred within the exposure area and properly aligned with the photoliths. The second subfigure of \autoref{fig:insol} shows a close-up view of the board and masks in the exposure area.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\subfigure[General view of the exposure unit]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\insolHeight]{figures/process_insolation_machine_open.jpg}}
	\subfigure[Close-up of the PCB with the photoliths]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\insolHeight]{figures/process_insolation_pcb_placement.jpg}}
	\caption{UV exposure unit with the PCB and photoliths ready}
	\label{fig:insol}
\end{figure}
%
Once positioned, close and clamp the exposure unit lid, then turn on the vacuum pump switch shown in \autoref{fig:insolClosed}. This draws a vacuum inside the exposure chamber to press the transparencies firmly and evenly against the board surfaces. Next, use the timer buttons to configure the exposure interval on the display. In this case, 150 seconds\footnote{Optimal duration depends on the specific exposure unit and the age of the photosensitive board.} was selected. Press the green ``START'' button to begin the exposure process, which cures/degrades the exposed photoresist. During this waiting time, it is advisable to proceed with the chemical preparation described in \autoref{section:prep}.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth]{figures/process_insolation_machine_closed.jpg}
	\caption{Exposure unit during the UV exposure process}
	\label{fig:insolClosed}
\end{figure}
%
When the timer completes, switch off the vacuum pump, release the clamps, and remove the copper board. A subtle contrast in colour will now be visible between the protected and exposed regions of the photoresist. Now proceed to the developing step in \autoref{section:soda}.
%
\section{Preparation of the developer and acid etchant}\label{section:prep}
During the UV exposure, it is good practice to prepare the developer (caustic soda) and the etching solution. The materials and safety equipment required are shown in the first subfigure of \autoref{fig:prep}.

Preparing the caustic soda solution requires only a suitable plastic container and sodium hydroxide dissolved in water\footnote{Always add the chemicals to water, never water to concentrated chemical crystals!}. The dark tint in the container shown in the second subfigure of \autoref{fig:prep} is because this developer batch has been reused several times.

The acid etching bath, on the other hand, is a stronger chemical blend and must not be stored long-term for repeated use. This transparent solution can be seen in the second subfigure of \autoref{fig:prep} and has the following composition by volume\footnote{Always wear protective nitrile gloves, safety goggles, and work in a well-ventilated area or fume cupboard}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Water $\Rightarrow$ 55\%
	\item Hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) $\Rightarrow$ 30\%
	\item Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) at 36\% $\Rightarrow$ 15\%
\end{itemize}

\section{Photoresist developing (caustic soda)}\label{section:soda}
Following the UV exposure described in \autoref{section:insol}, the board is immersed in the caustic soda developer bath. Agitate the board gently using soft plastic tweezers, taking care not to scratch the delicate photoresist layer.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\subfigure[Board immersed in the developer solution]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\insolHeight]{figures/process_soda_immersion.jpg}}
	\subfigure[Detail of the board developing]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\insolHeight]{figures/process_soda_detail.jpg}}
	\caption{Developing the exposed photoresist in the caustic soda solution}
	\label{fig:soda}
\end{figure}
%
The board should be removed from the developer once the tracks and pads appear sharply defined, as shown in \autoref{fig:aftersoda}. The dark areas retain the protective photoresist, while the exposed areas reveal the bare copper underneath.

Immediately rinse both sides of the board thoroughly under running water to neutralise and wash away any remaining caustic soda, then dry the board with absorbent paper towels.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{figures/pcb_result_after_soda.jpg}
	\caption{PCB appearance after developing in caustic soda}
	\label{fig:aftersoda}
\end{figure}

\section{Acid etching}\label{section:acid}
The etching procedure is similar to the developing step. Immerse the copper board into the tray containing the acid solution and agitate it gently using plastic tweezers or by rocking the tray smoothly\footnote{Exercise extreme caution with the acid mixture; work under an extraction hood!}, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:acid}.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\subfigure[Start of copper etching in acid]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\acidHeight]{figures/process_etching_start.jpg}}
	\subfigure[Agitating the board during etching]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\acidHeight]{figures/process_etching_progress.jpg}}
	\subfigure[Completed chemical etching of the copper]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\acidHeight]{figures/process_etching_complete.png}}
	\caption{PCB acid etching process}
	\label{fig:acid}
\end{figure}
%
The chemical proportions described in \autoref{section:prep} create a rapid, exothermic reaction. Keep the board moving continuously until all exposed copper has completely dissolved, matching the appearance shown in \autoref{fig:pcbs}. Inspect fine track details closely and do not leave the board in the acid longer than necessary, as over-etching will undercut traces.

After removing the board from the etching solution, rinse it under copious running water and pat dry with paper towels. The etched board should resemble the top and bottom sides of the finished boards shown in \autoref{fig:pcbs}.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\subfigure[Top layer view]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\pcbsHeight]{figures/pcb_result_etched_top.jpg}}
	\subfigure[Bottom layer view]{\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth, height=\pcbsHeight]{figures/pcb_result_etched_bottom.jpg}}
	\caption{Top and bottom views of a pair of etched PCBs}
	\label{fig:pcbs}
\end{figure}
%
Whereas the caustic soda developer can be reused several times, spent acid mixture must be stored in an approved chemical waste container, as shown in \autoref{fig:containers}, and collected by an authorised hazardous waste disposal contractor. Never pour etchant down standard municipal drains.
%
\begin{figure}[H]

	\label{fig:containers}
\end{figure}

\section{Post-processing and drilling}
%
Congratulations! Having reached this stage, you now have an etched, home-made PCB. It is now time to trim the board perimeter accurately if the initial cut did not achieve the required tolerances. You can use the guillotine shear once more, as depicted in \autoref{fig:postciz}, or use a rotary multi-tool (e.g. Dremel) fitted with a cutting disc\footnote{Always wear protective gloves, safety glasses, and a dust mask when cutting or drilling FR4 boards}.
%
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=.49\textwidth]{figures/process_cutting_post_etching.jpg}
	\caption{Trimming the finished PCB using the guillotine shear}
	\label{fig:postciz}
\end{figure}
%
Next, clean the board with acetone or isopropyl alcohol (IPA) to strip away the remaining photoresist mask from the copper tracks. 

The board must then be drilled for through-hole technology (THT) component leads, vias, and mounting holes. For this step, it is best to use a vertical drill press stand with a Dremel tool and appropriately sized solid carbide drill bits, as shown in \autoref{fig:taladrico}. A piece of scrap wood should be placed beneath the PCB to support the board cleanly and avoid chipping or tearing tracks on the exit side. \autoref{fig:drill} shows team members manually drilling boards using this setup.

\section{Soldering and assembly}
%
After completing the fabrication steps, the final stage is to solder the components onto the board. In this project, the components mounted on the boards shown in \autoref{fig:pcbsmain} include both surface-mount devices (SMD) and through-hole technology (THT) packages. Applying flux is highly recommended for both soldering types to promote proper solder wetting. To complete the assembly and protect the exposed copper against oxidation, apply a protective conformal lacquer or transparent solder-through protective varnish\footnote{Special solder-through flux sprays can be applied prior to soldering to protect the tracks whilst remaining solderable}.
